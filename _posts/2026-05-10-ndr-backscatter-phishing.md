While reviewing a case for a customer recently, I came across a pattern where the user received a phishing message that appeared to come from their own account and every layer of email authentication had passed cleanly. SPF, DKIM, and DMARC were all green. The attack wasn't sophisticated. The attackers exploited an RFC 5321 requirement that has existed since 1982: mail servers must deliver bounce notifications regardless of the original sender's authentication state.

## How the attack works

The mechanics in order: (1) attacker sends mail with forged sender to a guaranteed-to-bounce address, (2) recipient server generates NDR per RFC 5321, (3) NDR delivered to forged sender (the victim) with original message body intact, (4) attacker controls original message body — embeds phishing content, (5) victim receives NDR appearing to come from their own account.

Reference [RFC 5321](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/html/rfc5321)

The technical bypass becomes dangerous when combined with social engineering. Users rarely understand why they would receive an email from their own address, most interpret it as either account compromise or a forgotten send. When the message body contains instructions like "copy this link into your browser for security verification," users often comply, believing they're responding to a legitimate prompt from their own account. The result is credential harvesting via a fake login portal, with no malicious link ever clicked through traditional automated channels.

## Why standard mail filters miss it

Three reasons:
1. Authentication (SPF/DKIM/DMARC) applies to the inbound message from the third-party server, which is legitimate because the NDR is a real bounce. Authentication doesn't fail because there's nothing inauthentic about the bounce itself.
2. URL inspection often relies on parsing anchor tags. Plain-text URLs requiring manual user navigation bypass automated detonation entirely.
3. BATV (Bounce Address Tag Validation) would address this attack class by validating that bounces correspond to genuine outbound mail. Adoption is limited; Microsoft 365 doesn't implement it natively. 

## The detection logic

Four conditions: (1) NDR indicators in subject and sender, (2) presence of a plain-text URL, (3) manual-navigation instruction phrases in body, (4) SMTP enhanced status codes indicating recipient-unknown delivery failures.

The attacker deliberately targets non-existent recipients to guarantee bounce delivery, so the recipient-unknown error class is the canonical signature of this attack pattern.

The full rule is available in [my detection repository](https://github.com/LeRedMojo/ThreatHunting/blob/main/SIGMA/email/NDRVector.yml)

## Limitations

What the rule doesn't catch:
- Attackers who avoid common navigation phrases or use novel social engineering language
- NDRs generated by misconfigured legitimate senders may trigger the rule
- Sophisticated variants embedding the URL in attached message parts rather than body

Backscatter techniques predate most current defensive tooling, RFC 5321 was already two decades old before SPF, DKIM, and DMARC existed. Surfacing these patterns in modern SIEM detections is one small contribution to closing a gap that conventional email security doesn't address. Feedback from analysts running similar rules in production is welcome.
